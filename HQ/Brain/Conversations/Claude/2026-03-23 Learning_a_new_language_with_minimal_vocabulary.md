**Claude:** Yes, that's a well-established idea in linguistics! It's based on **word frequency analysis**, and the general principle holds up across most languages.

Here's how it roughly breaks down: the most common 100 words in a language typically account for about 50% of all spoken and written text, and the most common 300 words cover roughly 65%. If you push to around 1,000 words, you're looking at approximately 80-85% coverage of everyday conversation. The exact numbers vary by language, but the pattern is consistent — a small core of high-frequency words does a lot of heavy lifting.

There's a catch, though. "Coverage" doesn't mean "comprehension." Knowing 65% of the words in a sentence doesn't always mean you understand it — the missing 35% often carries the most meaning (nouns, verbs, adjectives specific to the topic). Think of a sentence like "Can you pass me the ___?" — the common words are easy, but the specific object is what matters.

That said, learning the most frequent 300–500 words is still one of the most efficient ways to start. It gives you a functional scaffold so you can begin picking up context, recognizing patterns, and filling in gaps naturally.

Why 200–300 words helps
Languages follow a frequency pattern: a small number of words show up all the time. If you learn:
* pronouns (I, you, we…)
* common verbs (go, eat, want, give…)
* connectors (and, but, because…)
* question words (who, what, where…)
* everyday nouns (food, time, place…)
…you can already understand a lot and form simple sentences.
The catch (especially in Bisaya)
Bisaya relies heavily on:
* verb focus/aspect (not just “eat” but “eating / ate / will eat / who is focused”)
* particles like na, pa, man, ba that add nuance
* flexible sentence order
So if you only memorize words without patterns, you’ll feel stuck quickly.
Good news: Bisaya is very learnable
Compared to many languages:
* No gendered nouns
* No complicated verb conjugation tables like Spanish
* Pronunciation is straightforward
A smarter approach (fast + practical)

1. Learn a “core set” of high-utility words

**Claude:** Great question — let me give you a straight comparison.
