**You:** If I borrow 100,000AUD and I have to repay 5% per month how much interest is this?

**Claude:** At 5% per month on a $100,000 AUD loan, the interest would be **$5,000 AUD per month**.

Over a full year (assuming simple interest with no compounding), that's $60,000 AUD in interest — which works out to a 60% annual rate.

If the interest compounds monthly, it's even higher. After 12 months you'd owe about $179,586 AUD, meaning roughly $79,586 in total interest — an effective annual rate of nearly 79.6%.

That's an extremely high rate. For context, typical personal loan rates in Australia are around 6–15% *per year*. If someone is offering you a loan at 5% per month, I'd strongly encourage you to look into other options — that kind of rate is often associated with predatory lending.

**You:** what about the philiphonnes

**Claude:** To put that 5% per month rate in perspective against what's normal in the Philippines:

- The Central Bank of the Philippines (BSP) recently cut its benchmark interest rate to 4.25% in February 2026 — that's 4.25% **per year**, not per month.
- The average bank lending rate was around 8% per year as of late 2025.
- Home loan rates from major banks like BDO, BPI, and Metrobank range from about 7–10% per year, with some digital lenders offering rates as low as 5.99% per year.

So even in the Philippines, where rates tend to be a bit higher than in Australia, a legitimate bank loan runs roughly **6–10% per year**. A loan at 5% *per month* (60% per year simple, ~80% compounded) would be many times higher than any regulated lending rate.

That kind of rate in the Philippines is typically associated with informal "5-6" lending (so called because you borrow 5 and repay 6), which is a well-known form of high-interest informal lending. It's legal in some forms but widely considered exploitative. If you're considering a loan like this, it would be worth exploring formal banking options or microfinance institutions, which offer far more reasonable rates.

**You:** what about angel investiing?

**Claude:** Great question — angel investing is a very different context from lending, so let me break this down.

**5% per month as an angel investing return?**

Angel investors don't typically charge monthly interest. Instead, they invest equity (buying a share of your company) and hope to make a return when the company succeeds — through a sale, IPO, or later funding round. The structure is fundamentally different from a loan.

Here's how the numbers typically work:

**Typical angel investing returns** tend to average around 20–25% per year for a successful portfolio, but this is highly uneven — most startups fail completely (losing 100% of the investment), while a small number deliver massive returns (10x, 50x, or more). Angels accept that risk in exchange for the potential upside.

**If someone is offering you "angel investment" at 5% per month interest**, that's not really angel investing — that's a high-interest loan dressed up with different language. A true angel investor takes equity and shares in the risk of the business. They don't get paid back monthly regardless of how the business performs.

**Common angel investment structures include** equity stakes (e.g. 10–20% of the company for a given investment amount), convertible notes (which convert to equity later, often with 5–8% *annual* interest), or SAFEs (Simple Agreements for Future Equity), which have no interest at all.

So if someone is calling themselves an "angel investor" but asking for 5% monthly repayments on a fixed amount, I'd be cautious — that's effectively a very expensive loan, not a true investment partnership. A real angel investor succeeds when *you* succeed, not by collecting guaranteed monthly payments.
